=== PRIMARY AND SECONDARY CATEGORIES (AI-Q definitions) ===

Pick ONE primary, then ONE secondary under it. Choose by the mechanism of harm, not by keywords.
The example in brackets shows how the category looks in a research paper.

Technical Risks: technical infrastructure, system performance, and technology reliability.
- Security Risk: attacks on the model, pipeline, or system, or exposure of credentials, secrets, or architecture.
  [A paper shows a prompt-injection attack that makes an agent leak its API keys.]
- Privacy Risk: personal or protected data exposed, inferred, or processed without basis.
  [A paper recovers training-set personal records from a model's outputs.]
- Technical/Performance Risk: model output quality failures: hallucination, inaccuracy, inconsistency, regressions after an update.
  [A benchmark shows accuracy falling 12% on inputs unlike the training data.]
- Data Risk: confidential, stale, or poisoned data mishandled or relied on.
  [A paper shows a few poisoned documents in a retrieval index change answers.]

Operational Risks: day-to-day business operations, processes, and workflow dependencies.
- Operational Risk: workflow disruption and process failure caused by the AI system.
  [An incident report describes an AI scheduling tool outage halting hospital shift planning.]
- Compliance/Regulatory Risk: AI-generated work or use that violates a regulation or required process.
  [A study finds AI-drafted clinical notes omit fields a regulation requires.]
- Legal/Liability Risk: legal exposure from AI output or AI-driven decisions.
  [A case report on an AI-drafted contract clause later found unenforceable.]
- Third-Party/Vendor Risk: dependency on an AI vendor's terms, pricing, availability, or changes.
  [An analysis shows an API deprecation breaking downstream products with no notice.]

Business Risks: strategic decisions, financial impact, and competitive positioning.
- Business/Financial Risk: IP loss, productivity loss, or direct financial harm.
  [A paper shows proprietary code can be extracted from a code assistant.]
- Reputational Risk: brand and trust damage from AI output or behavior.
  [A news report of a chatbot giving offensive answers to customers.]
- Ethical Risk: bias, unfair treatment, or harm to groups.
  [A study shows a hiring model ranks equally qualified women lower.]
- Strategic Risk: long-term positioning harm, skill atrophy, or market and regulatory shifts.
  [A survey finds teams relying on code assistants lose debugging skills over a year.]

Technical/Performance Risk is not a default. Use it only when the harm is the quality of
the model's output itself. A risk about workflow disruption is Operational Risk; a risk about
attacks is Security Risk; a risk about groups treated unequally is Ethical Risk.

=== EVIDENCE DISCIPLINE (read before extracting) ===

Sources are research papers, news reports, incident reports and vendor documents.
A record states only what the source supports. Four rules:

1. Measured vs guessed. Decide evidence_type before writing anything else:
   - "demonstrated": the source reports an observed result, experiment, or real incident.
   - "hypothesized": the source suggests, warns, speculates, or lists a limitation it did not test.
   - "mitigation_target": the risk is the problem the paper is trying to solve.
   Write demonstrated results in the past tense with their setting ("In tests on 3 models, X dropped 3%").
   Start speculation with "The authors suggest". Never turn "could" or "may" into "does".
2. Silence is not absence. Never state that a safeguard, test, or control is missing
   unless the source says so. If the source does not mention one, the only allowed
   value is "not described in source".
3. The solution is not the problem. If the paper proposes a method, defense, or tool,
   the risk is the problem the paper documents. Record the method in proposed_mitigation.
   Never attribute the problem to the proposed method.
4. Severity follows evidence. If evidence_type is "hypothesized", likelihood is at most 2.
   Every likelihood_reasoning and impact_reasoning sentence must contain a short phrase
   copied word for word from the source.

New fields (all required):
- source_type: "empirical_study" | "position_paper" | "survey" | "incident_report" | "news" | "vendor_doc"
- evidence_type: "demonstrated" | "hypothesized" | "mitigation_target"
- demonstrated_finding: what the source measured or observed, or null
- stated_implication: what the authors suggest could follow, or null
- paper_stance: "documents_problem" | "proposes_mitigation" | "evaluates_system" | "surveys" | "not_applicable"
- proposed_mitigation: the method the source proposes, or null
- mitigations_in_source: list of safeguards the source describes, or ["not described in source"]

=== RISK SCORING (LIKELIHOOD x IMPACT) ===

Score the extracted risk on two 5-point scales (NIST AI RMF / ISO 31000 style,
informed by the FAIR model). Base scores ONLY on evidence in the article.
Provide exactly ONE sentence of reasoning for each score.

LIKELIHOOD — probability the risk occurs or recurs. Consider FAIR Loss Event
Frequency factors: how often threat events like this occur, and how susceptible
the affected systems/population are.
1 = Rare — theoretical/speculative; no occurrence described and strong barriers exist
2 = Unlikely — could occur but described as an edge case or well mitigated
3 = Possible — plausible under normal conditions; near-misses or pilot findings reported
4 = Likely — incident already occurred once, or conditions make recurrence probable
5 = Almost Certain — recurring/widespread incidents, or a systemic flaw affecting all users

IMPACT — severity of consequences if the risk occurs. Consider FAIR Loss
Magnitude: the scale of primary and secondary losses.
1 = Negligible — minimal harm; easily reversible; no material, legal, or safety effect
2 = Minor — limited harm to few individuals or one organization; recoverable
3 = Moderate — meaningful harm: financial loss, privacy violation, or reputational damage
4 = Major — serious harm at scale: large breach, discrimination affecting many, major outage
5 = Severe — catastrophic: loss of life, critical-infrastructure failure, societal-scale harm

=== LOW-END SCORING ANCHORS ===

Low scores are correct when the evidence is limited. Use them.
- Likelihood 1: a theoretical concern with no experiment and no incident.
- Likelihood 2: shown only in a lab or simulated benchmark, or the authors call it an edge case.
- Impact 1: a limitation in a research setting with no deployment and no affected people.
- Impact 2: a small measured effect (for example a few percent accuracy) with no reported harm.

LOSS CATEGORIES — list ALL that apply from FAIR's six forms of loss, based on
the harms described or clearly implied in the article:
"Productivity", "Response", "Replacement", "Fines & Judgments",
"Competitive Advantage", "Reputation"

Rules:
- likelihood and impact must be integers 1-5, or null if the article gives
  no basis for a score. Use null rather than guessing.
- likelihood_reasoning / impact_reasoning: one sentence each, citing article evidence.
- Do NOT compute a combined severity number; it is derived downstream as
  likelihood x impact.
